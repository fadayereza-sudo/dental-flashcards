**Background**

When trauma occurs not only are the teeth involved, but also the child's entire self-image. Many problems may occur, even when the injury seems minor in nature, because of the varied response of the human dental pulp. Positive predictions on the future status of the injured tooth may be given with guarded optimism. No matter how sophisticated our diagnostic tools are the extent of injury to the pulp and surrounding tissues, blood vessels and nerves cannot always be accurately determined.

**Sequence of events**

1. Initial contact: speak to parent, get history of accident, try to alleviate anxiety.
2. Examine patient: quick clinical examination, ascertain extent of damage (?CNS involvement, etc.). Consider need to refer quickly for full medical appraisal; especially if there is a head injury.
3. Take radiographs at two different angles. Ideally record the injuries with photographs.
4. Check with parent to verify need for tetanus cover.
5. Consider the possibility of non-accidental injury (NAI).
6. Soft tissue examination and clean up.
7. Evaluation of extent of dental injury fracture, displacement, etc.
8. Immediate treatment, particularly considering pain control, protection of the pulp, repositioning and splinting of teeth.
9. Advice to child and parent regarding pain, swelling, discolouration.
10. Recall examination.
11. Produce accurate records.

The importance of good record keeping cannot be overstressed. There can be legal implications arising from accidents and the dentist could be asked to submit a report to an insurance company or a courtroom. Such reports should be comprehensive and outline the signs and symptoms presented at first appointment, treatment instituted and an estimation of treatment required to effect permanent restoration of the dentition.
