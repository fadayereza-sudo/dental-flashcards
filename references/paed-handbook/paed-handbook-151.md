**Enamel Defects**

- Defective enamel may exhibit either hypoplasia, due to deficient matrix production (quantitative defect) or hypomineralization from imperfect mineralization of the matrix proteins (qualitative defect).
- Hypoplastic enamel may be thin, grooved or pitted.
- Hypomineralised enamel may be mottled or have opacities.
- The main clinical characteristics will govern the classification of the anomaly, but combinations of the two usually co-exist, especially at the histological level.

**Genetically determined enamel defects:**

- Those that are unassociated with a systemic abnormality are known as amelogenesis imperfecta (AI).
- Those that are associated with generalized disorders:
  - There are a number of uncommon genetically determined diseases where defective enamel formation seems to be part of the condition.
  - Included are conditions such as epidermolysis bullosa, tuberous sclerosis and tricho-dento-osseous syndrome for example.

**Environmentally determined enamel defects:**

These may be caused by a systemic or local factor.

- **Systemic**
  - Those related to systemic upset will be related to the timing of the problem.
  - The part of the tooth affected will be that that was developing at the time of the insult; the defect will be chronologically arranged around the arch.
- **Localized**
  - Infection or trauma may result in enamel defects.
