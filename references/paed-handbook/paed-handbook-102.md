**Introduction**

Despite the reduction in the extent and severity of dental caries in primary teeth in England, Wales and Northern Ireland in the last decade, large proportions of children remain affected by the disease (Child Dental Health Survey 2013, 2015). It is therefore, important for dental students to be familiar with the techniques for restoring carious primary teeth.

Inflammation of the pulp in primary molars develops at an early stage of interproximal caries attack and by the time interproximal caries involvement presents clinically with marginal ridge breakdown, pulp inflammation is quite extensive. Hence, early appropriate restorative treatment should be provided for carious primary teeth with consideration of the state of the primary pulp (Duggal et al., 2002; Kassa et al., 2009).

Two treatment approaches are available for the management of carious primary teeth in the UK; conventional and biological:

- The **conventional approach** consists of complete removal of carious tissue followed by placement of a suitable filling material such as composite resin, amalgam or a stainless steel crown (SSC) with or without pulp therapy. Pulp therapy includes both pulpotomy and pulpectomy.
- The **biological approach** involves partial caries removal followed by placement of a suitable filling material as seen in indirect pulp capping (IPC) or sealing dental caries in place by means of SSC and its cement (Hall technique).

Once pulp therapy has been carried out in primary molars, they become brittle and prone to fracture. In many cases a considerable amount of the crown will have been lost and it is then difficult to retain an amalgam or composite filling. Any restoration that is placed should have the same lifespan as that remaining for the tooth and should also protect the remaining tooth structure. In these cases, a stainless steel or nickel chromium crown is the treatment of choice.
