## The recommendation

Reduce the use of dental amalgam in line with the UK regulations implementing Article 10(2) of EU Regulation 2017/852 on Mercury, in support of the global Minamata Convention to cut environmental mercury pollution.

## Why

The phase-down is driven by the environment, not by patient harm. Reviews from SCENIHR, the UK COT and the FDA all conclude that mercury exposure from amalgam restorations does not adversely affect patient or operator health. The problem is what happens to the mercury after it leaves the surgery. Non-organic mercury from amalgam waste, cremation and burial is converted by aquatic microorganisms into methylmercury, the bioaccumulative form that contaminates fish and re-enters the human food chain. Globally, around two-thirds of the mercury in amalgam is eventually released into the atmosphere, soil and water, and dental amalgam was estimated to account for 20–30% of EU mercury demand in 2010.

## How this applies in practice

- When patients ask whether amalgam is dangerous to them, the honest answer is no — the restriction is environmental, not toxicological.
- The phase-down is enforced through the Control of Mercury (Enforcement) Regulations 2017, with existing dental regulators as the competent authorities — non-compliance is a regulatory matter, not just guidance.
- Three concrete legal duties flow from Regulation 2017/852: pre-dosed encapsulated amalgam only (Article 10(1), from 1 Jan 2019), restricted patient groups (Article 10(2), from 1 Jul 2018), and amalgam separators in every dental facility (Article 10(4), from 1 Jan 2019).
