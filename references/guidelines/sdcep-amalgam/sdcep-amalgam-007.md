## The recommendation

Do not remove or replace existing amalgam restorations in children, pregnant or breastfeeding women as a precaution against the new restrictions. SCENIHR's position is that "there is no justification for removing clinically satisfactory amalgam restorations as a precaution, except in those patients diagnosed as having allergic reactions to amalgam constituents".

## Why

The mercury vapour release from an amalgam restoration is highest during placement and removal. Removing a clinically sound restoration just to comply with the spirit of Article 10(2) actually increases the patient's mercury exposure, with no offsetting clinical benefit. The Article 10(2) restriction is forward-looking — it stops new amalgam going in, it does not require old amalgam to come out. Allergy to amalgam constituents is the one diagnosed indication for removal, and even then it is the diagnosis that drives the decision, not a precautionary impulse.

## How this applies in practice

- A patient who asks to have all their amalgams replaced "because of the new rules" needs a careful explanation that removal would expose them to more mercury, not less, with no proven health gain.
- Recognised indications for amalgam removal remain: failed restoration, recurrent caries, fracture, diagnosed allergy to amalgam constituents.
- Pregnant patients: defer any non-urgent removal until after delivery.
- The same logic protects you medico-legally: prophylactic removal of sound amalgam is hard to justify if the patient later complains.
