## The recommendation

When treating permanent teeth in a patient under 15, avoid amalgam unless justified by the specific clinical circumstances or needs of the patient — examples include allergy or local adverse reaction to a component of glass-ionomer or resin composite, or when moisture control or patient cooperation is insufficient to allow an alternative even as a medium-term restoration.

## Why

Resin composite and high-viscosity glass-ionomer can restore even large or extensive cavities effectively, but only if moisture control is achievable and the patient can tolerate the appointment. The two recognised failure modes for alternatives are biological (allergy to composite or glass-ionomer components, which is uncommon but real) and practical (a child who cannot keep still or be isolated long enough for adhesive technique to work, or whose attendance for replacement is uncertain). In those genuine cases, an amalgam restoration that lasts is better for the patient than a failing composite that needs revisiting.

## How this applies in practice

- Decide before you pick the material: can you get this tooth dry, and will this patient cope with the procedure required by the alternative? If both answers are yes, amalgam is not justified.
- Factors to weigh when considering an exception: patient age, prognosis of the tooth, additional needs, likelihood of attendance for further treatment.
- Document the specific reason — "moisture control unattainable on lower right second molar in an uncooperative 12-year-old" beats "amalgam used at clinical discretion".
- Take valid consent from parent/carer (and child where appropriate) covering the rationale and the alternatives considered.
