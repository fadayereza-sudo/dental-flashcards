## The recommendation

A patient who has ever taken a bisphosphonate is allocated to a risk group as if they are still taking it, regardless of how long ago they stopped.

## Why

Bisphosphonates have a high affinity for hydroxyapatite and become incorporated into bone. Alendronate's half-life in bone is approximately 10 years, so a patient who finished a five-year course three years ago still has substantial drug embedded in skeletal tissue. There is also evidence the drugs impair soft tissue healing and inhibit angiogenesis, which compounds the osteoclast-suppression effect at extraction sites. The cumulative dose, not the current dose, drives risk — which is why treatment for more than five years pushes osteoporosis patients into the higher-risk category.

## How this applies in practice

- Ask "have you ever taken a bisphosphonate?" rather than "are you on a bisphosphonate?" — a "no" to the second question often hides a yes to the first.
- A drug holiday or a stopped course does not reset the clock.
- For osteoporosis patients on bisphosphonates more than five years (the SIGN review point), reclassify to higher risk.
- Anti-angiogenics, by contrast, are not thought to persist — once stopped, the added risk fades quickly.
