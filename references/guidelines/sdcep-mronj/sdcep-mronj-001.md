## The recommendation

Three drug classes are implicated in MRONJ: anti-resorptives (bisphosphonates and the RANKL inhibitor denosumab) and anti-angiogenic agents. Both oral and intravenous routes carry risk.

## Why

Anti-resorptives suppress osteoclast-driven bone turnover; the jaw, with its high remodelling rate, is disproportionately affected. Bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, zoledronate, pamidronate) bind hydroxyapatite and persist in bone for years. Denosumab (Prolia 60 mg for osteoporosis; Xgeva 120 mg for cancer) is a monoclonal antibody against RANKL with the same osteoclast-suppressing endpoint but no skeletal reservoir. Anti-angiogenics (bevacizumab, sunitinib, aflibercept) impair vascular ingrowth needed for healing and add to the risk when combined with anti-resorptives.

## How this applies in practice

- Always ask specifically about these drugs at every medical history update — patients often do not realise an annual zoledronate infusion or six-monthly denosumab injection counts as ongoing therapy.
- Calcium and vitamin D supplements alone are not anti-resorptive and do not place a patient at MRONJ risk.
- Brand names matter: Prolia (osteoporosis) and Xgeva (cancer) are both denosumab; the same drug carries different risk because the cancer dose and frequency are higher.
- New oncology agents with anti-angiogenic action keep appearing — check the SDCEP/MHRA list before assuming a drug is unrelated.
