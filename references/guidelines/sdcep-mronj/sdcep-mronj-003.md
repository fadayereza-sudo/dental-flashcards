## The recommendation

A patient who has had denosumab in the last nine months is allocated to a risk group as if they are still taking it. After nine months from the last dose, the bone-turnover effect has resolved and they are no longer considered at risk on this basis.

## Why

Denosumab is a monoclonal antibody against RANKL that is not incorporated into bone, so its osteoclast-suppressing effect is reversible as the antibody is cleared. Bone turnover diminishes back toward baseline within nine months of the last injection. This is the single biggest practical difference from bisphosphonates: denosumab risk is time-limited, bisphosphonate risk is essentially permanent.

## How this applies in practice

- For an osteoporosis patient on six-monthly subcutaneous denosumab, the practical window for non-urgent invasive dental treatment is the month before the next scheduled dose, when serum drug level is lowest. Liaise with the prescriber and delay the next denosumab injection until the socket has healed.
- For a cancer patient on monthly Xgeva, this windowing is not feasible — treat them as continuously at higher risk.
- A patient who stopped denosumab a year ago for unrelated reasons no longer carries denosumab-attributable MRONJ risk; check whether they had bisphosphonates before that, since bisphosphonate risk does carry over.
