## The recommendation

In the mixed dentition, take an intraoral radiograph for failure of incisor eruption, missing or peg-shaped lateral incisor, retained deciduous predecessors, or unpalpable maxillary canines from age 11. Use a panoramic when one or more first permanent molars have a doubtful prognosis or when multiple developmental anomalies are suspected.

## Why

Each indication maps to a treatment decision that hinges on knowing what is — or is not — present in the bone. Failure of incisor eruption is most often caused by a supernumerary or trauma sequel; the radiograph determines whether to refer for surgical exposure or simply observe. A peg-shaped lateral or unilateral retained deciduous canine is a recognised marker for an ectopic permanent canine; finding the canine early lets interceptive extraction of the deciduous canine straighten its path. Unpalpable canines at 11 mean it is time to stop guessing — late diagnosis of an ectopic canine is associated with root resorption of the adjacent incisors and a much harder later orthodontic problem. A doubtful first molar (e.g. molar–incisor hypomineralisation, gross caries) needs a panoramic before the orthodontist plans balancing extractions, because the second premolar must be present to take the space.

## How this applies in practice

- Children are radiosensitive — every exposure must be specifically justified, never routine.
- Always check existing bitewings first; they often show the second premolars and unerupted canines you are about to image for.
- Palpate buccal sulcus for canines at every paediatric exam from age 9 onwards. By age 11, an unpalpable canine triggers an intraoral.
- Use the lowest-dose appropriate view: an intraoral over a panoramic, a field-limited panoramic over a full one.
- For complex orthodontic radiographic decisions, refer the child rather than expose them in general practice — the choice belongs to the specialist.
