## The recommendation

Use paediatric-specific exposure protocols in children because their radiation-induced cancer risk is two to three times that of adults in their thirties for the same dose. In pregnancy, no dental view requires deferral (only vertex occlusals point at the abdomen), but offer pregnant patients the option of delaying non-urgent radiography.

## Why

Radiation risk is age-dependent because younger tissues are more radiosensitive and the latent period between exposure and tumour is shorter than the child's remaining life expectancy. Children therefore need the lowest reasonably practicable dose — which usually means smaller sensors, shorter exposure times, and skipping marginal exposures. For the foetus, dental x-ray beams are pointed at the head and neck, not the abdomen, so scatter dose to the foetus is negligible. The reason to still offer deferral is emotive, not biological: pregnant patients and their partners worry, and a later miscarriage or malformation (background rates apply regardless of x-rays) can be wrongly attributed to the radiograph if one was taken against the patient's instinct.

## How this applies in practice

- Never use an "adult" exposure setting on a child — drop the time/mA per the machine's paediatric protocol.
- Vertex occlusal radiographs are the only dental view aimed at the abdomen and are rarely indicated; if one is essential in pregnancy, use abdominal lead.
- For panoramic, CBCT, periapical and bitewing in pregnancy: no radiobiological need to delay. Document the conversation and the patient's preference.
- The very elderly: risk is effectively negligible because life expectancy is shorter than the latency period. Do not let fear of radiation keep you from taking a radiograph an elderly patient clinically needs.
