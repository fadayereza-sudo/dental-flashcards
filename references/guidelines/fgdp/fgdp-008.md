## The recommendation

Every endodontic case requires at least one good-quality pre-operative periapical for diagnosis and planning, a working-length radiograph (unless an electronic apex locator gives confident readings), at least one post-operative periapical immediately after obturation, and a follow-up periapical at one year to assess healing.

## Why

A pre-op periapical reveals everything that affects whether and how to treat: number and curvature of roots, pulp chamber and canal anatomy, periapical lesion size and shape, proximity of antrum/IDC/mental foramen, restorability of the crown, and existence of prior root filling. Without it you are operating blind on root morphology and may perforate, ledge, or miss canals. The working-length radiograph (or apex locator confirmation) prevents over-instrumentation past the apical constriction, which causes severe periradicular inflammation. The immediate post-op view is the baseline against which one-year healing is judged: a reduction in periapical radiolucency at one year predicts long-term success and triggers discharge from review.

## How this applies in practice

- Pre-op periapical is non-negotiable. If the first view does not show all roots clearly, take a second with 10–20 degrees of horizontal angulation to separate them by parallax.
- Apex locators are reliable, but radiographically confirm working length when there is fluid in the canal, a metallic post nearby, or a large apical foramen — all of which degrade locator accuracy.
- The "diagnostic file" working-length radiograph: if the file is more than 3 mm from the planned working length, adjust and re-radiograph before continuing.
- One year post-op: if the lesion is reducing, no further imaging needed. If unchanged, re-radiograph at two years before considering retreatment.
- A baseline periapical is mandatory before pulp capping or pulpotomy and after all but minor dental trauma; for trauma, follow-up at six months then annually until root formation completes.
