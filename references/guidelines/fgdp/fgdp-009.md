## The recommendation

CBCT is not a routine method for caries diagnosis, periodontal assessment, root-canal anatomy, or implant planning. Limited-volume, high-resolution CBCT is justified only when conventional 2D imaging is equivocal or inadequate and the additional 3D information will alter management — for example, suspected resorption, complex endodontic anatomy, surgical endodontic planning, or implant sites where the inferior dental canal or sinus floor cannot be confidently localised.

## Why

CBCT delivers 5 to 100 times the dose of a periapical, and metallic restorations produce streak artefacts that mimic or mask caries. Beam-hardening from amalgams and crowns is severe enough that CBCT can introduce artefactual radiolucencies on adjacent teeth. The technique's strength — three-dimensional imaging — only justifies the dose when 3D information would change the plan. For most dental questions, intraoral or panoramic radiography with appropriate technique answers the clinical question at a fraction of the dose. Use of CBCT as a "defensive" or "I want a better look" investigation is exactly the kind of routine practice the European evidence-based criteria were written to stop.

## How this applies in practice

- Caries diagnosis: never use CBCT. Stick to bitewings.
- Endodontic resorption (suspected internal or external): limited-volume high-resolution CBCT is appropriate.
- Mandibular third molar with panoramic suggesting close root–IDC relationship: a localised CBCT may change surgical management. Routine CBCT for all third molars is not supported and there is no evidence of better outcomes.
- Implant planning where periapical or panoramic shows inadequate bone height/width to safely place, or where the IDC, mental foramen, or antral floor needs three-dimensional localisation: CBCT is appropriate.
- Always limit field of view to the question being asked. Full-volume scans need to be reported by a suitably qualified radiologist because they include the skull base.
