## The recommendation

For single-implant treatment planning use a periapical (with rectangular collimation and paralleling technique); add a panoramic for posterior or multiple sites; reserve limited-volume CBCT for cases where the inferior dental canal, mental foramen, antral floor, or buccolingual ridge cannot be confidently assessed in 2D. Take a baseline radiograph at completion of the prosthodontic phase and review at 12 months, then up to every five years.

## Why

Periapicals give the highest resolution and lowest dose for the proposed implant site, with a paralleling technique limiting magnification error to ±2.5 mm — adequate for most single-tooth replacement decisions. Their weakness is that they are 2D: you see height, but not buccolingual width or the third-dimension position of the IDC. Panoramics fill in some of that gap but magnify by up to 30% with variable distortion along the arch. CBCT is the only modality that gives true cross-sectional information at a manageable dose, so it is reserved for the cases where 2D cannot answer the question. Routine post-implant follow-up is for monitoring marginal bone loss — the early sign of peri-implantitis — and the long interval (up to 5 years) reflects how slowly stable implants change.

## How this applies in practice

- For an uncomplicated anterior single tooth: periapical with a paralleling holder is enough.
- Posterior mandible implants need at least 2 mm (preferably 4 mm) safety margin above the IDC; if the panoramic does not show this clearly, escalate to limited-FOV CBCT.
- Use a metallic ball bearing in a stent or denture during the planning radiograph to calibrate magnification.
- After placement, the baseline view goes in at the prosthodontic completion appointment. Routine immediate post-op imaging is only justified if there are surgical complications or doubt about implant position.
- Bitewings already taken for caries can double as marginal-bone-level checks for posterior implants — no extra dose needed.
