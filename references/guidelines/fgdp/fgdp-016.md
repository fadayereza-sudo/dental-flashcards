## The recommendation

When justifying or counselling about dental x-rays, frame the dose against natural background radiation: a periapical equates to up to three and a half days of background, a panoramic to no more than six days, a CBCT scan to between two days and six months depending on the machine, and a chest x-ray to about three days. The risk of a cancer being induced from 1 mSv of head radiography is approximately 1 in 15,000 (men) and 1 in 18,000 (women) for a 30–39-year-old.

## Why

Patients (and clinicians) wildly overestimate the risk from dental radiography because the word "radiation" carries non-quantitative dread. Anchoring the dose to the background radiation everyone receives just by being alive (about 2,230 µSv per year in the UK) lets the patient calibrate. The lifetime cancer risk numbers are real but tiny — a single periapical adds risk on the order of one in a million — and frame the absolute, not relative, scale of harm. The UK Health Protection Agency estimates diagnostic radiology causes about 700 cancers per year nationally, only a small fraction from dental sources, so the collective dose from dental imaging is genuinely low. The combination of the absolute risk being small and the diagnostic benefit being substantial is the reason justification, not abstinence, is the operative principle.

## How this applies in practice

- Patient asks "how much radiation is in this x-ray?" — "About the same as you'd get walking around for three or four days" is an honest, low-jargon comparator.
- Anxious patient declining a clinically indicated radiograph: the comparison to background, to a transatlantic flight (about 80 µSv), or to a chest x-ray often unblocks the decision.
- Risk is not zero: the linear-no-threshold model assumes any dose carries some risk, so "ALARP" — as low as reasonably practicable — applies to every exposure even when the absolute risk is tiny.
- Patient with health anxiety or specific concern (history of cancer, family fear): document the discussion and the decision either way.
- Pregnant patient: the foetal dose from any dental view (vertex occlusal excepted) is essentially undetectable above background.
