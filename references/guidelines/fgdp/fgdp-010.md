## The recommendation

For the new adult patient, take periapical views of all teeth that are root-filled or heavily restored (direct or indirect). Only teeth with extreme toothwear need periapical investigation; routine radiography of teeth worn into dentine is not justified.

## Why

Apical periodontitis can develop without symptoms in heavily restored teeth, and 19–36% of root-filled and crowned teeth have a periapical radiolucency on radiographic survey. The strongest predictors of asymptomatic apical pathology are the presence of a root filling and the presence of three or more inadequate coronal restorations. Catching these lesions on a baseline periapical lets you plan retreatment before symptoms emerge or the tooth fails. Toothwear is different: 99% of severely worn teeth have no apical pathology because the slow rate of dentine exposure lets the pulp lay down tertiary dentine in defence. Only when wear is "extreme" (rapid, into pulp territory) is screening worthwhile.

## How this applies in practice

- New adult exam: take a periapical of every root-filled tooth, every crowned tooth, and every tooth with a large or compromised direct restoration.
- "Heavily restored" with direct fillings is a judgement call — large MOD, deep cervical restoration, multiple surfaces with marginal defects all qualify.
- Toothwear: only image teeth with extreme wear (rapidly progressive, near-pulpal) or where the patient reports symptoms. Periodic review radiographs of stable worn teeth are not needed unless symptoms develop.
- Document the justification for each periapical individually in the notes — "root-filled UR6, asymptomatic, screening for apical periodontitis" rather than a generic "PA UR6".
- Pre-extraction: there is no convincing evidence to support routine pre-extraction radiography in adults. Selective indications only — previous difficult extractions, suspected unusual anatomy, medical-history risk, prior to orthodontic extractions, or impacted/buried roots near important structures.
