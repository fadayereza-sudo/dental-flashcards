## The recommendation

Panoramic radiography is not a routine examination. It is only justified when a specific clinical indication is present: a bony lesion or unerupted tooth too large to demonstrate on intraoral views, a grossly neglected dentition needing multiple extractions, assessment of third molars before planned surgery, or an orthodontic assessment where tooth count or position must be established. There is no justification for review panoramic radiography at arbitrary intervals.

## Why

Panoramic radiography is inferior to intraoral radiography for diagnosing the common dental pathoses (caries, periodontal, periapical disease): the image is blurred by the rotating geometry, the focal trough distorts anything outside a narrow slice, and the magnification varies along the arch. The dose per exam is higher than a bitewing. Routine panoramic screening in asymptomatic adults yields few clinically significant findings, the same logic that ended medical "whole-body" screening decades ago. Using a panoramic as a "safety net" exposes every patient to extra dose for the benefit of the rare anecdotal finding in a handful.

## How this applies in practice

- Do not offer a "baseline panoramic" to every new adult patient — this is explicitly called out as unjustified.
- Before surgical removal of a mandibular third molar, a panoramic is appropriate; at the initial assessment visit it is not.
- If a panoramic suggests close relationship of a lower third molar root to the inferior dental canal, a second view with different projection geometry — or a limited-FOV CBCT — may change surgical management. Routine CBCT for third molars is not supported.
- For TMJ clicking without other signs or symptoms, do not radiograph. Panoramic adds nothing to the management of most TMD.
- Always pair a panoramic with periapical or bitewing supplementation when specific detail is needed — the panoramic alone is rarely sufficient for treatment planning.
