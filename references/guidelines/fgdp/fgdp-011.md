## The recommendation

Grade every radiograph against the three-point quality scale: Grade 1 (excellent — no errors of exposure, positioning, or processing), Grade 2 (diagnostically acceptable — errors that do not detract from diagnostic utility), Grade 3 (unacceptable — errors that render the image diagnostically useless). The PHE audit targets are at least 70% Grade 1 and no more than 10% Grade 3.

## Why

Without a standardised quality measure there is no way to know whether radiation dose is being wasted on uninterpretable films. A Grade 3 radiograph means the patient was exposed for nothing and the exposure must usually be repeated, doubling the dose. The targets are pitched to be ambitious but achievable — 70% excellent allows for genuinely difficult patients (strong gag reflex, anatomical variation), and capping unacceptable at 10% forces investigation of any operator, processor, or sensor that drifts higher. CBCT is held to a stricter standard (no more than 5% diagnostically unacceptable) because metallic artefact prevents an "excellent" grade and the dose stakes are higher.

## How this applies in practice

- Grade and record every radiograph in the notes at the time it is taken — alongside justification and clinical findings, this is the IRMER documentation triad.
- Audit at least once a year: take a sample of 20 radiographs per operator, grade them, identify the failure modes (positioning, exposure, processing, sensor), and feed back to the team.
- Do not let a DCP rubber-stamp every image as Grade 1 — anecdotally common, and a regulator who spots it will assume your wider QA is similarly weak.
- Record the operator's name on every exposure so audit can target individual training rather than blame the team.
- A repeat rate climbing above 10% is the audit signal to act before a regulator does.
