## The recommendation

Intraoral x-ray equipment should operate at 60–70 kV, use constant-potential generators, have adequate aluminium filtration, a long focus-to-skin distance (≥200 mm), an open-ended spacer cone with rectangular collimation, and a fast image receptor (F-speed film or digital sensor).

## Why

Each element targets a different part of the dose–benefit equation. Higher kV (60–70) delivers a lower-energy penumbra to patient tissues while still producing diagnostic contrast; constant potential removes the low-energy "waste" photons that shed dose without contributing to the image. Rectangular collimation confines the beam to the actual shape of the receptor, cutting irradiated tissue area by up to 60% compared with a round cone. A long focus-to-skin distance reduces skin-entrance dose by the inverse square. F-speed film or a digital detector needs a fraction of the exposure older D-speed film required. Combined, these can produce a tenfold difference in effective dose for the same image.

## How this applies in practice

- If your set only allows a round collimator, a rectangular adaptor clips over the cone and is cheap. This is the single biggest dose-reduction change available.
- Never use D- or E-speed film in 2026 — F-speed or digital only.
- Use a paralleling holder (Rinn or equivalent) every time; free-hand bisected-angle technique wastes dose on repeats.
- The 2010 national survey found the average adult molar dose had fallen 57% since 1999 purely through equipment and receptor upgrades — meaning any dentist still on old kit is well above the diagnostic reference level.
- Digital does not automatically mean lower dose: the wider dynamic range tempts operators to overexpose. Use the lowest exposure that answers the clinical question, not the one that gives the prettiest image.
