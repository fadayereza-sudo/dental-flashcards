## The recommendation

Advise families to confine sugary foods and drinks to meal times, drink only water or milk between meals, avoid sugary drinks or milk in bottles and pacifiers, and not eat or drink anything (except tap water) after the evening brush.

## Why

The dose of sugar matters less than the frequency: every sugar exposure triggers acid production, and enamel needs hours to remineralise between attacks. Grazing or sipping sugary drinks keeps the mouth below critical pH almost continuously, so lesions initiate and progress. Confining sugar to meal times collapses the number of acid attacks per day; milk is effectively non-cariogenic so it's the safe between-meal drink. Sugary bottles at bedtime are the classic early-childhood-caries mechanism — saliva flow drops during sleep, so prolonged sugar contact with the upper incisors goes unchallenged. Not eating after the night brush preserves the fluoride film (the "spit don't rinse" effect depends on it).

## How this applies in practice

- Standard Prevention: dietary advice given at least once a year, including meal-time-only sugar, milk/water only between meals, no sugary drinks at bedtime, awareness of hidden sugars in yogurts/cereals/crisps.
- Sugar-free alternatives for long-term medications — contact the GP or pharmacist to swap cariogenic liquids.
- Fresh fruit is fine; fruit juice and smoothies are cariogenic (sugar is released from the fibre structure).
- Enhanced Prevention for increased-risk children: at every recall, plus consider a 3–5 day food and drink diary (including one weekend day) or a 24-hour recall diary in the surgery.
- Label thresholds worth teaching parents: high sugar is >22.5 g/100 g in food or >11.25 g/100 ml in drinks; low is ≤5 g/100 g or ≤2.5 g/100 ml.
- Cow's milk from 12 months, semi-skimmed only after 2 years when diet is varied; skimmed only after 5 years.
- Acidic drinks (including sugar-free fizzy drinks) contribute to erosion — meal times only.
