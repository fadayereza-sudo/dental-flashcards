## The recommendation

Do not carry out complete caries removal on advanced dentinal lesions in primary molars — use selective caries removal or the Hall Technique instead, because complete removal carries a significantly higher risk of pulp exposure.

## Why

Primary molar pulps are large with high pulp horns, so a complete-removal bur working toward the pulpal floor is operating in a very narrow margin. Selective caries removal leaves soft dentine over the pulp — starved of substrate under a well-sealed restoration, the residual bacteria stop driving progression and the lesion arrests. The Hall Technique achieves the same biological seal without any caries removal at all. Both approaches preserve vitality and exfoliation timing; complete removal trades restoration longevity (unnecessary in a tooth with 5 years left) for a pulp exposure that means pulpotomy or extraction.

## How this applies in practice

- Advanced occlusal lesion in a primary molar: selective caries removal + adhesive restoration, or Hall Technique if cooperation/multi-surface involvement favours it.
- Advanced proximal lesion in a primary molar: Hall Technique first choice (also avoids iatrogenic damage to the adjacent first permanent molar). Alternative is selective caries removal + adhesive restoration.
- Complete removal remains appropriate in primary anterior teeth where aesthetics demands it (e.g. strip-crown restoration of an upper A) and in permanent teeth with moderate lesions where surface area for bonding is limited.
- If a lesion has no clear dentine band to the pulp on radiograph and the child has no pulpitis symptoms, Hall (with consent about the uncertain prognosis) is safer than attempting complete removal.
- Do not use dental amalgam in primary teeth — regulation and environmental factors have made it second-line at best; composite, RMGI, compomer, GI, or strip crowns are the restorative choices.
- Don't use conventional glass ionomer for multi-surface cavities in either dentition — too high a failure rate.
