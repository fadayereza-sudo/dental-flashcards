## The recommendation

For a patient on single or dual antiplatelet therapy, treat without interrupting the medication. Aspirin alone is managed as a routine low-bleeding-risk case; any other single antiplatelet, or a dual combination, needs tighter planning because bleeding may be prolonged for up to an hour.

## Why

Aspirin monotherapy produces a bleeding risk that is close to non-anticoagulated baseline, so routine extractions often do not need sutures. Clopidogrel alone, prasugrel or ticagrelor (newer, more potent) and any dual therapy (typically aspirin with clopidogrel, prasugrel or ticagrelor for acute coronary syndrome or recent stenting) all genuinely prolong primary haemostasis. Dual therapy bleeding rates remain low overall (0.4–3.8% across studies) and are controllable with local measures. Stopping these drugs, however, is dangerous: stent thrombosis rates spike if dual therapy is interrupted in the first 12 months post-stent. The recommendation is strong because the thromboembolic harm of stopping outweighs the low-certainty bleeding risk of continuing.

## How this applies in practice

- Aspirin alone: treat like any patient, limit the initial area, stage higher-risk work, achieve haemostasis with local measures — sutures often not required.
- Clopidogrel alone, prasugrel, ticagrelor, or any dual therapy: expect bleeding time up to an hour and plan the appointment around that. Limit the initial area, stage higher-risk work, suture and pack every socket.
- Patients with a coronary stent in the last 12 months are on dual therapy that must not stop without cardiology's written say-so — absolute do-not-interrupt group.
- Most common combination: aspirin + clopidogrel (post-ACS). Aspirin + dipyridamole (after stroke/TIA) is rarer now, as clopidogrel monotherapy has replaced it.
- Watch for OTC aspirin in a patient who is already on clopidogrel — effectively converts to dual therapy.
- Book for a longer slot and early in the day so you can watch haemostasis before discharge.
