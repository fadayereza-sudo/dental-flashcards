## The recommendation

In elderly patients, cap ibuprofen at 1,200 mg/day, co-prescribe a PPI, and avoid diclofenac; reduce doses and consider renal, cardiac, and GI risk before any NSAID.

## Why

Older patients accumulate the things that make NSAIDs dangerous: declining GFR means less renal prostaglandin reserve to spare when you block COX; age-thinned gastric mucosa bleeds more easily; cardiovascular disease is commoner so the thrombotic NSAID risk lands on an already elevated baseline. Falls in renal function are often subclinical until the NSAID precipitates AKI. Halving the dose and covering the stomach is a standard mitigation; diclofenac stays off the menu because its cardiovascular signal is too strong for the at-risk group.

## How this applies in practice

- Default elderly analgesic plan: paracetamol 1 g QDS first; add ibuprofen only if needed, cap at 1.2 g/day, prescribe PPI alongside, limit to 5 days.
- Diclofenac is not recommended in the elderly in this guide — cardiovascular risk too high.
- Benzodiazepines (diazepam for TMD): halve the adult dose.
- Monitor blood pressure, renal function, and heart failure signs 1–2 weeks after starting or increasing an NSAID — liaise with the GMP to arrange.
- Check the actual medication list, not just the condition list — many elderly patients are on a drug that interacts without realising.
- Consider weight-appropriate paracetamol dose reduction below 50 kg.
