## The recommendation

For patients with accelerated tooth wear, identify extrinsic acid sources — carbonated drinks (except plain sparkling water), wines, alcopops, smoothies, energy drinks, fruit juices (orange, grapefruit, lemon, blackcurrant), fruit teas, sports drinks, vinegar-based foods, acidic sweets, and acidic medications — and limit them to mealtimes. Substitute with plain water, plain sparkling water, milk, or unsweetened tea/coffee.

## Why

Drinks have the highest erosive potential of all dietary sources, and timing/frequency matters more than the single dose. Confining acidic intake to meals lets saliva buffer and clear the acid alongside food, and stops the all-day pH drop that comes from sipping. Plain water, plain sparkling water, milk, tea and coffee have the lowest erosive potential — sparkling water without flavouring is fine for teeth despite being slightly acidic. Fruit teas and "natural fruit juices" sound healthy to patients, but in this context they behave the same as soft drinks.

## How this applies in practice

- Confine acidic drinks to mealtimes, not "throughout the day".
- Distinguish fruit *juice* (mashed fruit pulp) from fruit *drink* (sugar/colour/flavour with little real juice) — both are erosive but patients often think the second is safer.
- Carbonated water with fruit flavouring or lemon is highly erosive — recommend plain sparkling water if fizz is wanted.
- Reducing the temperature of a hot fruit tea slows erosion, but swapping to camomile or peppermint (non-fruit herbal) is better.
- For acidic medications (chewable vitamin C, aspirin, asthma inhalers, iron preparations) discuss swapping to swallowable forms or non-acidic alternatives with the GP/pharmacist.
- Don't forget occupational exposure (wine tasters, acid mists, swimming-pool workers) — ask if wear is unexplained by diet.
