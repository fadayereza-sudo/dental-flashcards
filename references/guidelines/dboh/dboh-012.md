## The recommendation

Use the OARS communication strategy in behaviour-change conversations: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening, and Summarising.

## Why

Behaviour-change advice fails when the patient feels lectured. OARS is a structured way of running the conversation so the patient does the talking and the clinician's role is to draw out, validate and reflect what the patient already half-knows. Open questions ("how do you feel about your brushing?") give the patient room to explore their own position rather than answering yes or no. Affirmations recognise effort and the patient's own perspective, which builds the rapport that makes them willing to change. Reflective listening shows you have heard the answer and lets the patient hear it back. Summarising at the end consolidates the agreed plan into something the patient owns rather than something you imposed.

## How this applies in practice

- "Open" beats "closed": "What is your usual brushing routine?" beats "Do you brush twice a day?"
- An affirmation is recognising effort, not flattery: "It's not easy fitting brushing in with shift work — it's really good that you've kept the morning brush going."
- Reflective listening is repeating back the meaning, not the words: "So you're saying mornings work but the night brush gets dropped when you're tired."
- Summarising at the end of the appointment, including what the patient committed to, makes the plan stick.
- If you are doing more than half the talking, you are not running an OARS conversation.
