## The recommendation

Infants should be exclusively breastfed for around the first 6 months of life. Breastfeeding up to 12 months is associated with a decreased risk of dental caries and may offer some protection compared with infant formula. Once teeth erupt, brush as close to bedtime as possible rather than waking the child.

## Why

Breast milk contains lactose (not a free sugar) and immunological factors that, in the under-12-months window, are associated with reduced caries risk in the deciduous dentition. The picture beyond 12 months is murkier — much of the evidence is observational and confounded by other dietary, hygiene and fluoride exposures. The pragmatic message is therefore: breastfeed if you can, brush erupted teeth as bedtime nears, and don't worry about feeding patterns disrupting sleep.

## How this applies in practice

- Breastfed babies still need their teeth brushed once they erupt — start brushing the moment the first tooth appears.
- If a baby falls asleep at the breast, brush as close to that bedtime as practical; do not wake them to brush.
- Be breastfeeding-friendly in the dental setting: a private space, accommodating appointment timing for nursing parents.
- Don't tell mothers to stop breastfeeding for caries reasons under 12 months — the evidence does not support that.
- Beyond 12 months, evidence is limited; focus the conversation on tooth brushing, fluoride, and free-sugar intake from solids and other drinks.
- Bottle-fed or mixed-fed: the same brushing rules apply, plus the additional bottle-feeding precautions (nothing but milk, formula, or cooled boiled water in the bottle).
