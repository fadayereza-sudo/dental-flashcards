## The recommendation

Do not apply fluoride varnish in patients with ulcerative gingivitis or stomatitis, and check medical history for severe asthma requiring hospitalisation or for allergy to sticking plaster (a marker for colophony allergy) before applying colophony-containing varnish.

## Why

The most commonly used fluoride varnishes contain colophony (a pine-resin derivative) as the carrier. Colophony is a known contact and respiratory allergen — the cross-reaction with sticking-plaster adhesive is the standard pre-screen because both share rosin chemistry. In a patient with severe asthma, an allergic reaction to inhaled or absorbed varnish components could trigger a hospital-grade attack in the chair. Ulcerative mucosal lesions strip the protective epithelium, so varnish application becomes painful and allows much greater systemic absorption of fluoride and resin than intended.

## How this applies in practice

- Two pre-application questions every time: "Have you ever had a bad allergic reaction or hospitalisation for asthma?" and "Any allergy to sticking plasters?"
- Look in the mouth before applying — visible ulceration or acute necrotising gingivitis means defer.
- Different brands have different bases; if a patient genuinely needs varnish but cannot have colophony, source a colophony-free product rather than skipping varnish indefinitely.
- Document the medical-history check in the notes each visit — this is the contraindication most likely to be missed in a busy recall appointment.
