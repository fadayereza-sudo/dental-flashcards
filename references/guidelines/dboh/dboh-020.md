## The recommendation

Where fluoride therapy is not possible, consider chlorhexidine varnish (1% or 10%) applied 3-monthly by a clinician to exposed root surfaces in older adults or patients with xerostomia.

## Why

Root caries lesions sit on dentine, which demineralises at a higher pH than enamel and is harder to remineralise. In patients who cannot tolerate fluoride or whose risk is dominated by dysbiotic plaque (e.g. severely xerostomic care-home residents), an antimicrobial that suppresses cariogenic bacteria offers an alternative angle. Limited evidence from a small number of trials suggests chlorhexidine varnish reduces both new-lesion initiation and the activity of existing root caries lesions when applied 3-monthly. The evidence is weaker than for fluoride varnish, so the agent is positioned as a fallback, not a first-line option.

## How this applies in practice

- First-line for root caries remains the full fluoride bundle: 1,350–1,500 ppm or higher paste, fluoride varnish, daily 0.05% NaF rinse.
- Reach for chlorhexidine varnish when fluoride is contraindicated, refused, or not delivering control (e.g. patient with severe xerostomia from radiotherapy, retained roots in a frail older adult, persistent active root caries despite optimal fluoride).
- Apply 3-monthly to the exposed root surfaces; this is a clinician-applied product, not patient self-care.
- Avoid in patients with chlorhexidine allergy — anaphylaxis is rare but documented.
- Monitor for the predictable chlorhexidine side effects when used long term: extrinsic staining, taste disturbance, mucosal soreness.
