## The recommendation

Distinguish age-appropriate wear from non-age-related (accelerated) tooth wear, then focus management on identifying and removing risk factors before the wear progresses. Screen for tooth wear in young children, because dentine-exposing wear in primary teeth predicts wear on permanent first molars at age 12.

## Why

Tooth wear is a normal cumulative process, so the same BEWE score means very different things in a 20-year-old and an 80-year-old. Severity must be judged against the patient's age. Acting on accelerated wear early is the only realistic preventive lever — once tissue is lost it does not regenerate, so the goal is to slow or stop progression and to find the causative behaviour or condition before more enamel is gone. Identifying accelerated wear in childhood also opens a window onto undiagnosed eating disorders, GORD, and dietary patterns that affect overall health.

## How this applies in practice

- Compare what you see to age expectations: 4% of 16–24 year-olds have moderate wear; 44% of 75–84 year-olds do.
- A 25-year-old with BEWE 3 is a red flag — investigate diet, reflux, vomiting, bruxism, and brushing habits before assuming "they're just getting older".
- Screen primary teeth — wear with dentine exposure in 5-year-olds predicts permanent first-molar wear at 12.
- Use the term "tooth wear" with patients (drop "tooth surface loss") and avoid "abfraction" — there is no clinical evidence it is a separate aetiology.
- Where you find accelerated wear, the discussion may surface mental-health issues (eating disorders, anxiety driving bruxism). Plan for a longer, sensitive consultation and a supportive referral pathway.
