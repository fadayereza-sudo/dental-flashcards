## The recommendation

Recognise oral HPV infection as a major risk factor for oropharyngeal cancer; support the school HPV vaccination programme for boys and girls; counsel patients that HPV is mainly transmitted via sexual contact, including oral sex.

## Why

The fastest-rising oral cancer in the UK is oropharyngeal, and the rise tracks closely with HPV-positive disease. HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancers tend to present at a slightly younger peak age (61–65) than oral-cavity cancers and have a different risk profile — patients may be non-smokers and non-drinkers and so feel falsely reassured by their lifestyle. The prevention story has three legs: vaccination of children (the only true primary prevention; benefit will take decades to show in cancer rates), counselling about sexual transmission, and continued vigilance for symptoms in patients without classic tobacco/alcohol risk.

## How this applies in practice

- Do not "rule out" oropharyngeal cancer in a non-smoking, non-drinking patient with persistent throat symptoms — HPV-driven disease can present in this group.
- The HPV vaccine is offered to UK schoolchildren of both sexes; encourage uptake when families ask, and document the patient's vaccination status if known.
- Sexual-history conversations are usually the GP's territory, but a brief note that HPV can be transmitted through oral sex is appropriate when explaining risk.
- Symptoms suggestive of oropharyngeal involvement — persistent sore throat, hoarseness, dysphagia, neck lump — warrant urgent referral.
- The patient demographic for HPV+ oropharyngeal cancer is shifting younger; ageist screening assumptions ("she's only 45, can't be cancer") miss diagnoses.
