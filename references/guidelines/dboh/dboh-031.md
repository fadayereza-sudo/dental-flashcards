## The recommendation

Stage periodontitis by maximum interproximal bone loss as a percentage of root length: Stage I (<15% or <2 mm), Stage II (coronal third), Stage III (mid third), Stage IV (apical third); describe the extent as localised (≤30% of teeth), generalised (>30%), or molar-incisor pattern.

## Why

Stage describes the historical degree of breakdown — how much bone has already been lost — and is therefore irreversible: the stage cannot decrease, only stay the same or increase. It captures the severity that drives prognosis and treatment complexity. Splitting the root by thirds turns a continuous radiographic measurement into four reproducible categories that any GDP can read consistently from a panoramic or bitewing. The extent descriptor adds the spatial pattern: a localised molar-incisor pattern in a young patient implies aggressive susceptibility, while generalised Stage IV in a 70-year-old usually means decades of slow accumulation.

## How this applies in practice

- Measure on bitewings or periapicals from CEJ to the most apical extent of bone loss; if no radiographs are clinically justified, measure attachment loss in millimetres from the CEJ.
- A patient who has lost teeth to bone loss likely within the apical third can be assigned Stage IV directly.
- Stage IV implies tooth loss or a complex restorative challenge ahead — these patients usually need referral or co-management.
- Localised vs generalised is decided by whether more than 30% of teeth show the worst stage observed.
- Molar-incisor pattern in a young adult or adolescent is a red flag for aggressive disease — refer, and screen first-degree relatives.
