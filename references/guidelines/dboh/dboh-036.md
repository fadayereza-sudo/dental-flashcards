## The recommendation

Distinguish three anatomical groups when describing or coding oral cancer: oral cavity (mouth), oropharynx (throat), and outer lip; recognise that they have different epidemiology, risk factors and management pathways.

## Why

The three groups behave clinically as separate diseases. Oral cavity cancers (inner lip, tongue body, floor, palate, gums, buccal mucosa) are dominated by tobacco and alcohol risk; oropharyngeal cancers (base of tongue, lingual tonsil, tonsil, oropharynx) are increasingly HPV-driven and rising rapidly in incidence; outer lip cancer is largely UV-driven and far less common. Lumping them together as "mouth cancer" obscures these differences and can lead to misjudged risk assessment. The distinction also matters for referral — head and neck specialists triage by site, and the GDP's job is to identify which compartment a lesion sits in.

## How this applies in practice

- "Tongue cancer" needs to be split: anterior two-thirds (oral cavity) vs base of tongue (oropharyngeal) — they have different risk factors and treatment.
- A lesion on the inner lip is oral cavity cancer; on the outer vermilion border it is lip cancer with a UV history relevant.
- Oropharyngeal cancer is now more common in Wales than oral-cavity cancer and is the fastest-rising cancer in Scotland — peak age 61–65.
- Around 56% of mouth-cancer patients survive 5 years; oropharyngeal is slightly better at 66%; survival is strongly dependent on stage at diagnosis.
- Document the precise site of any concerning lesion in the referral letter — not "oral cancer suspected" but "ulcerated lesion left lateral border of tongue."
