## The recommendation

Recognise that for oral cancer risk, frequency and amount of alcohol over a few years matters more than total duration in years; higher consumption over a short period carries higher oral cancer risk than lower consumption over many years. Alcohol and tobacco act synergistically — combined use multiplies risk far beyond their individual contributions.

## Why

Alcohol is a known carcinogen for cancers of the mouth, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, liver, breast and bowel. The oral cancer dose-response is driven by recent intensity, not lifetime exposure — the tissues seem to respond more to recent damage than to cumulative damage. Tobacco and alcohol share metabolic pathways: alcohol increases mucosal permeability to tobacco carcinogens, while tobacco impairs mucosal repair from alcohol-induced injury. The result is a multiplicative, not additive, risk for head and neck cancer.

## How this applies in practice

- A patient who drinks heavily for a short period and smokes is at far higher oral cancer risk than the simple sum of those two factors suggests — say so plainly.
- Don't reassure patients on the basis of "only a few years" — recent intensity matters most for oral cancer.
- Pharyngeal/oral-cavity cancer is more strongly tied to drink-years than laryngeal cancer.
- Routine oral cancer screening at recall (chapter 6) is especially important in combined alcohol + tobacco users.
- If a patient is quitting smoking, addressing alcohol at the same time gives bigger oral-cancer-risk reduction than tackling either alone.
