## The recommendation

Identify patients on long-term liquid, chewable or soluble medicines, ask whether they are sugar-free, and write to the GP requesting a sugar-free alternative where one is listed in the BNF.

## Why

Long-term liquid medications can deliver a substantial daily sugar load, often missed because patients do not classify medicines as a dietary item. Frequent dosing means the teeth see acid challenges every few hours throughout the day, on top of any dietary sugar. The risk concentrates in the populations least able to compensate: children with chronic illness (epilepsy, recurrent infections), older adults on polypharmacy, people on methadone (commonly the sugar-based formulation despite a sugar-free version existing), and care-home residents on sugary nutritional supplements. Sugar-free alternatives — fructose, glucose and sucrose all absent, or replaced by sorbitol, xylitol, maltitol or hydrogenated glucose syrup — are non-cariogenic.

## How this applies in practice

- For any patient on long-term liquid medicine, check the BNF for a sugar-free version and write to the GP if one is available — a downloadable template letter accompanies DBOH.
- If no sugar-free alternative exists, advise the patient to take the medicine at mealtimes where possible, when salivary clearance is highest.
- Common offenders to flag: lactulose and morphine sulphate oral solution (top-5 prescribed in England, both sugar-based), sugar-based methadone, paediatric paracetamol/ibuprofen suspensions, antibiotics.
- Care-home residents on milkshake-style oral nutritional supplements: changing the supplement is rarely realistic, so escalate fluoride (high-ppm paste, varnish, mouth rinse) instead.
- Polypharmacy in older adults is doubly cariogenic — sugar in the medicine plus xerostomia from anticholinergics, antidepressants and antihypertensives.
