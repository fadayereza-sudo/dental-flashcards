## The recommendation

The safest approach for women who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy is not to drink alcohol at all. Drinking in pregnancy can lead to long-term harm to the baby; the more she drinks, the greater the risk. Risk is likely to be low if only small amounts were consumed before she knew she was pregnant.

## Why

There is no established safe threshold for alcohol in pregnancy. Alcohol crosses the placenta freely and can affect fetal neurodevelopment at any stage; the dose-response relationship is not linear and individual susceptibility varies. The pragmatic public-health position is therefore zero alcohol in pregnancy. The reassurance for inadvertent low-volume exposure before pregnancy is recognised exists to avoid unnecessary anxiety, not to license continued drinking once pregnancy is known.

## How this applies in practice

- For pregnant patients, deliver the message clearly without scolding: "The safest amount in pregnancy is none."
- Reassure those who drank before they knew: "If it was a small amount, the risk is low. Stop now."
- Free dental care during pregnancy is a window to deliver health-behaviour advice that primary care may have missed — alcohol fits alongside smoking cessation here.
- For partners and friends: don't push drinks on a pregnant patient — relevant in alcohol-rich social cultures.
- Document the conversation; offer onward referral to specialist services if the patient is dependent and pregnant.
