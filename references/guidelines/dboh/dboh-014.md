## The recommendation

Treat behaviour change as a cycle running from unaware, through thinking about change, to planning, acting and maintaining; meet the patient where they are on the cycle rather than always pushing for action.

## Why

Patients who are not yet aware that there is an issue, or who are still weighing the pros and cons of change, will not benefit from action-focused advice. Trying to push them straight to "act" fails and damages rapport. Stages of change is non-linear — patients can slip back and revisit earlier stages, and many take several attempts before a new behaviour holds. Recognising which stage the patient is in tells the clinician which intervention to use: information for the unaware, motivational dialogue for the contemplative, SMART planning for the prepared, support for the active, relapse-prevention for the maintaining.

## How this applies in practice

- A smoker who shrugs off cancer risk is in pre-contemplation; raise the issue, do not nag, and leave the door open: "I'll ask again next time."
- A patient saying "I know I should cut down on sugar in tea" is contemplating; explore pros and cons, build motivation, do not jump to a plan yet.
- A patient asking "how do I floss properly?" is in preparation; this is the moment for SMART goals.
- A patient who has changed but has slipped at Christmas is relapsing; normalise it, reframe as data not failure, restart.
- Pick the one behaviour the patient says they are most ready to change, even if it is not the one you would prioritise — readiness wins over priority every time.
