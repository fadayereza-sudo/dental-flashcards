## The recommendation

Recognise reduced salivary flow as a major risk multiplier for tooth wear and address it directly. Wide-bore drinking straws, positioned towards the back of the mouth, may be considered as part of risk management, alongside avoidance of holding or swilling acidic drinks.

## Why

Saliva is the body's principal defence against erosive challenges: it dilutes, buffers, clears acid, and supports remineralisation. Any condition or medication that suppresses flow lengthens the period the tooth is exposed to acid after each intake. A wide-bore straw positioned posteriorly minimises contact between the drink and the teeth, but the evidence is weak — drinking behaviour and the pleasure of taste mean people often defeat the protective intent. It's a reasonable adjunct, not a primary intervention.

## How this applies in practice

- Identify medications that reduce salivary flow (anticholinergics, antidepressants, antihistamines, diuretics, opioids) — they are common and easily missed.
- Encourage patients to seek GP review for non-essential xerostomic medications and to consider saliva substitutes (sprays, gels, pastilles).
- Where erosive drinks cannot be eliminated, advise a wide-bore straw aimed to the back of the mouth — flag that this only helps if the drink is not held or swilled.
- For patients with persistent dry mouth, consider sugar-free gum or lozenges to stimulate residual flow at risk times.
- Frequent post-exercise dehydration plus sports/energy drinks is a high-risk combination — discuss alternatives with patients in regular intense exercise.
