## The recommendation

Both powered and manual toothbrushes are effective for plaque control. Moderate-certainty evidence shows powered brushes reduce plaque and gingivitis more than manual brushes in the short and long term, but the clinical relevance is unclear; either type can clean teeth effectively.

## Why

The Cochrane evidence favours powered brushes statistically, but the absolute differences are small enough that affordability, preference, and technique matter more than brush type for most patients. There is no evidence that any particular powered mode (sonic vs rotatory) is superior, and no evidence on powered-vs-manual for caries prevention specifically. For periodontal patients and those with limited dexterity, the consistent motion of a powered brush often improves outcomes; for an able patient with good technique, a manual brush is fine.

## How this applies in practice

- Don't push expensive powered brushes on patients who can't afford one — say outright that a manual brush used well cleans teeth effectively.
- Powered brushes are particularly worth recommending for: patients with dexterity issues, periodontal patients, those who consistently miss areas with a manual brush.
- Pressure sensors on modern powered brushes help patients who scrub hard — a useful selling point in tooth-wear cases who choose powered.
- For tooth wear specifically: there's no evidence powered is better or worse than manual — patient preference wins.
- People with learning disabilities may benefit from a powered brush, but some find the vibration distressing — try before recommending.
