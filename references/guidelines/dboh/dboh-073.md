## The recommendation

NRT, varenicline and bupropion all improve quitting chances. Combination NRT (a patch plus a fast-acting product) is more effective than single NRT and equally effective as varenicline. Higher-dose 21 mg/24-hour patches outperform lower-dose 14 mg patches in those motivated to quit. Bupropion is contraindicated in patients with seizures.

## Why

NRT works by replacing nicotine without the combustion products of tobacco, dampening withdrawal so behavioural change can take hold. Single NRT alone covers either steady or burst cravings; combining a patch (steady baseline) with gum, lozenge, inhalator or spray (burst rescue) covers both — hence the additive effect. Varenicline (Champix) is a partial nicotine-receptor agonist that blunts both cravings and the reward of any cigarette smoked during the attempt. Bupropion reduces urges but lowers seizure threshold, which is why a seizure history rules it out.

## How this applies in practice

- Recommend combination NRT as the default OTC route — single NRT (one form alone) is significantly weaker.
- For pregnant patients: NRT may help and is routinely used; evidence is low-certainty but there is no signal of harm.
- All licensed NRT forms (gum, patch, nasal spray, inhalator, sublingual tablets/lozenges) increase quit rate by 50–60%; choice is patient preference.
- Varenicline is usually first-line on prescription unless contraindicated — refer to GP/pharmacist for prescription.
- Varenicline side effects: nausea, insomnia, abnormal dreams, headaches; small concern about cardiovascular events in already at-risk patients.
- Pharmacotherapy is much more effective combined with behavioural support than alone — refer in parallel.
- No evidence supports pharmacotherapy in children; NRT is licensed in the UK from age 12.
