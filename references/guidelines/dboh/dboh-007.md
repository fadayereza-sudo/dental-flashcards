## The recommendation

Treat "strong" recommendations as defaults the GDP departs from only with documented reason; treat "conditional" recommendations as advice to weigh against the individual patient; treat "good practice" points as expert consensus where no robust evidence exists.

## Why

DBOH grades every recommendation using GRADE so that the GDP can read the table and know, at a glance, how much latitude they have. A "strong" recommendation means the Guideline Development Group is highly confident the desirable consequences outweigh undesirable ones across the whole patient population, usually backed by high or moderate certainty evidence. A "conditional" recommendation means the GDG is less confident — either the evidence is low or very low certainty, or the harm/benefit balance is unclear — so individual patient factors carry more weight. "Good practice" points exist where research is lacking but expert consensus and precedent agree the action is sensible. A conditional grade does not mean the intervention does not work; it means the certainty is lower.

## How this applies in practice

- Strong recommendations (e.g. twice-yearly fluoride varnish, twice-daily 1,000 ppm+ brushing) are the standard of care — skipping them needs justification in the notes.
- Conditional recommendations (e.g. avoid sugars at bedtime in adults, daily fluoride mouth rinse) are still worth offering, but expect to negotiate them with patient preference.
- Good practice points (e.g. liaise with GP about sugar-free alternatives) cannot be cited as evidence-based, but they are the agreed best behaviour in their absence.
- When defending a clinical decision in audit or complaint, naming the recommendation grade shows you understood why you did or did not follow the guideline.
