## The recommendation

Treat combined heavy tobacco and alcohol use as multiplicative — over a 10-fold increase in oral cancer risk versus people who abstain from both.

## Why

Tobacco and alcohol act synergistically rather than additively. Alcohol thins the mucosal barrier and acts as a solvent, increasing the local penetration of tobacco carcinogens; tobacco delivers the genotoxic insult; together each amplifies the other's effect on the oral epithelium. The pooled epidemiology converges on >10× risk for heavy users of both. There is no safe lower threshold — risk increases with any level of either, but the combination is the catastrophic exposure pattern. Smoking duration is more important than frequency (years of exposure outweighs daily count), while alcohol frequency outweighs duration (a few years of heavy daily drinking is worse than a long period of light drinking).

## How this applies in practice

- The highest-risk patient in the chair is the smoker who also drinks daily — flag explicitly in the notes.
- For a patient who smokes 5/day for 30 years vs 30/day for 5 years, the longer duration is worse for oral cancer risk despite identical pack-years.
- For alcohol, "more than 3 drinks per day for a few years" carries more risk than "1 drink per day for many years."
- Quitting smoking lowers risk over many years but never to never-smoker level — frame quitting as risk reduction not erasure.
- Use the Very Brief Advice approach: ask, advise, act — strong recommendation at every visit, not just initial assessment.
