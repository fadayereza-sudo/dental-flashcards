## The recommendation

Recognise that erosive potential is not predicted by pH alone. Increased erosion occurs with lower pH, lower salivary flow and buffering capacity, higher titratable acidity, lower calcium and phosphate content, lower fluoride content, and higher drink temperature.

## Why

Unlike caries, dental erosion has no single critical pH threshold, because mineral content (especially calcium) can blunt the erosive effect even of an acidic drink. Titratable acidity matters more than pH — it measures how much buffer is needed to neutralise the drink, so a drink with low pH but few free H+ ions can be less erosive than expected, and vice versa. Saliva is the body's main defence: it dilutes, buffers, and clears acids and re-mineralises afterwards. Anything that reduces saliva, or any habit that prolongs contact (swilling, holding, sipping), magnifies the damage.

## How this applies in practice

- Calcium-fortified juice or milk-based drinks erode less than equivalent-pH plain juice — the calcium reduces dissolution.
- Warm fruit teas are more erosive than cold ones; a patient sipping hot lemon water through the morning is doing more damage than the pH alone suggests.
- Patients with low salivary flow (medications, anxiety, intense sport, Sjögren's) are at much higher erosive risk for the same drink intake.
- Habits matter more than total volume: swilling, holding, or sipping over 30 minutes overwhelms saliva's protective capacity.
- Fluoride in saliva and toothpaste also reduces dissolution — keep fluoride exposure high in any erosion patient.
