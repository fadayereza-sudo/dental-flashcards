## The recommendation

Refer suspicious lesions to the head and neck specialist for scalpel biopsy and histological assessment; do not use vital staining, oral cytology, light-based detection or oral spectroscopy to evaluate lesions for malignancy.

## Why

Histology of an excisional or incisional scalpel biopsy is the only diagnostic test with the sensitivity and specificity to confirm or exclude malignancy. Adjunctive technologies — toluidine blue vital staining, brush cytology (OralCDx), light-based devices (VELscope, ViziLite) — were marketed as triage aids, but the systematic-review evidence does not support their use for evaluating clinically evident lesions. They produce false reassurance (a negative adjunct test in a clinically suspicious lesion is dangerous) and false alarm (positive results in benign lesions). Sending the patient to a specialist for a definitive answer is faster, safer and more accurate than chasing intermediate results.

## How this applies in practice

- A clinically suspicious lesion goes on the urgent suspected cancer pathway, not into a brush-biopsy or VELscope adjunct workflow.
- Do not buy or use light-based or vital-staining devices as a diagnostic shortcut — DBOH explicitly advises against them.
- Incisional biopsy of clinically suspicious lesions is best done by the specialist team — sampling error is meaningful and the specialist will want to choose where to take the bite.
- For a homogenous leukoplakia that is clearly low-risk (e.g. frictional from a sharp cusp), removing the irritant and reviewing in 2–3 weeks is reasonable; if it persists, refer.
- "I used the [adjunct device] and it was negative" is not a defence if a missed cancer is later diagnosed — the device is not validated for that use.
