## The recommendation

Grade periodontitis by the ratio of % bone loss to patient age: Grade A (slow) if <0.5, Grade B (moderate) 0.5–1.0, Grade C (rapid) >1.0.

## Why

Grade describes the speed of breakdown — how fast disease has accumulated relative to time of exposure — and identifies the patients whose susceptibility outpaces the average. A 30-year-old with 40% bone loss has a ratio of 1.33 (Grade C), telling you the patient is a rapid progressor regardless of how much bone has gone yet; a 70-year-old with the same 40% loss has a ratio of 0.57 (Grade B), telling you the disease has accumulated at a typical rate. Grade is what decides how aggressive maintenance care must be and whether smoking or diabetes are propelling the disease faster than expected. Grade can be stepped up in light of risk modifiers like smoking and uncontrolled diabetes.

## How this applies in practice

- For each patient, find the worst site of bone loss as a % of root length, divide by age, and grade A/B/C.
- A young adult with Grade C disease needs urgent intensive care plus a search for risk drivers — smoking, undiagnosed diabetes, family history.
- A Grade A disease in an older patient can usually be managed in primary care with standard SPC.
- Grade can be modified upwards by current smoking ≥10/day or HbA1c ≥7.0% — these accelerate progression.
- Document stage and grade together, e.g. "Generalised Stage III Grade B periodontitis, currently stable."
