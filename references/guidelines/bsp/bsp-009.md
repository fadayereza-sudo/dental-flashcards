## The recommendation

Use the 1999 Armitage classification to assign a periodontal diagnosis. Chronic periodontitis is the slow plaque-driven destruction of the supporting tissues affecting roughly half the adult population, with severe forms in 10–15%. Aggressive periodontitis is rapid attachment loss in roughly 1 in 1000 patients, usually under 35 years, often with little plaque relative to the destruction, sometimes vertical bone defects on radiographs, and often a family history.

## Why

The two categories are distinguished by the *rate* of progression and the *match* between plaque levels and tissue destruction. Chronic periodontitis tracks the amount of plaque — a lot of plaque produces a lot of disease. Aggressive periodontitis breaks that rule: severe destruction in a young, otherwise healthy patient with minimal calculus and a positive family history points to a host susceptibility issue (often genetic) that needs specialist input. Recognising the difference matters because aggressive periodontitis has a worse prognosis, may benefit from adjunctive systemic antibiotics, and should be referred for specialist care; treating it as ordinary chronic perio loses time and teeth.

## How this applies in practice

- For every patient with attachment loss, match plaque levels against the destruction. Heavy plaque + matching destruction = chronic. Light plaque + severe destruction in someone under 35 with a family history = aggressive — refer.
- Vertical (angular) bone defects on a young patient with little calculus are a red flag for aggressive periodontitis even before formal diagnosis.
- Aggressive periodontitis can be localised (some teeth) or generalised (all teeth) — both warrant referral.
- If a patient with no other risk factors presents with multiple periodontal abscesses, consider undiagnosed diabetes or other systemic disease driving the breakdown — refer for medical screening.
