## The recommendation

Treat tobacco use as the single most important modifiable periodontal risk factor: ask about it at every recall, document current self-reported status and historic burden in pack-years, warn that even 1–4 cigarettes a day raises periodontitis risk by almost 50%, and offer brief cessation advice or referral to a service such as NHS Smokefree.

## Why

Smokers are up to six times more likely than non-smokers to show periodontal destruction, lose teeth twice as often in the long term, and respond more poorly to periodontal therapy. The mechanism is a drop in gingival blood flow, impaired neutrophil function, impaired wound healing, and increased inflammatory cytokine production — so smoking both drives the disease and blunts the response to treatment. The dose–response is steep enough that even very light smoking matters. Importantly, reduced gingival blood flow masks bleeding on probing, so a smoker's chart can look misleadingly healthy.

## How this applies in practice

- Ask and document smoking status at every recall, plus pack-years (packs per day x years smoked).
- For smokers thinking about quitting, refer to NHS Smokefree; for those who decline, give brief cessation advice yourself or delegate within the team.
- Warn cessation patients that bleeding on probing may transiently increase as gingival circulation normalises and the masking effect lifts — this is healing, not a relapse.
- It takes on average seven serious attempts to quit; treat each "failed" attempt as a milestone and reopen the conversation at the next visit.
- For smokers already in periodontal care: avoid surgical or grafting procedures because of the impaired healing capacity, and refer non-responders rather than persisting with repeated debridement.
- Smokers with recession are at high risk of root caries — pair perio care with dietary advice and a high-strength fluoride toothpaste.
