## The recommendation

Recognise peri-implant disease in two forms: peri-implant mucositis (gingivitis-equivalent — inflammation of the peri-implant tissues with bleeding on probing but no bone loss, ~45% of patients) and peri-implantitis (periodontitis-equivalent — progressive loss of alveolar bone support around the implant, ~20% of patients). Prevent it by meticulous peri-implant oral hygiene and risk-factor control; treat mucositis by professional and patient plaque removal; refer suspected peri-implantitis for specialist management.

## Why

Implants are as susceptible to bacterial plaque as teeth, but the peri-implant tissues lack a periodontal ligament and have a weaker collagen-fibre attachment, so disease spreads faster and is harder to arrest. Peri-implantitis can progress more rapidly than periodontitis, which is why early detection by routine probing and radiographic monitoring matters. Patients who could not keep their teeth clean enough to avoid losing them are unlikely to manage implants any better — and any non-modifiable factor that drove their original periodontal breakdown (genetics, immune dysregulation) will continue to operate around the implant. Hence implants are not a cure for periodontitis; they are a substitute for an absent tooth.

## How this applies in practice

- Probe every implant at least annually — four or six points — and record BOP and suppuration. This is your screening tool, not the BPE.
- Take baseline periapicals at implant placement and superstructure delivery to give a real reference for future comparisons.
- Mucositis (BOP, no bone loss): improve plaque control with single-tufted and interdental brushes; reinforce risk-factor control; reassess.
- Peri-implantitis (BOP/suppuration with bone loss): refer to specialist — treatment may involve non-surgical or surgical debridement of the implant surface with adjuncts. Counsel the patient that affected implants are more likely to be lost even with treatment.
- A history of severe periodontitis is not an absolute contraindication to implants but it is a strong amber flag — counsel the patient about higher rates of integration failure and peri-implantitis.
- "I did not place it" is not a defence for missing peri-implantitis at a recall — anyone who sees the patient is expected to assess and record it.
