Some endodontic problems and their management

Acute periapical abscess

Relief of symptoms requires drainage of the abscess and where possible this should be obtained through the tooth under a rubber dam. Open the pulp with a diamond bur in a turbine handpiece while supporting the tooth to ↓ vibration. Regional anaesthesia and occasionally sedation may be required. Once opened, the canal is irrigated with sodium hypochlorite and, if at all possible, resealed. It may be necessary to see the patient again in 24h, rather than leaving the tooth on open drainage. Relieve any traumatic occlusion.

If a fluctuant abscess is present this should be incised. If drainage can be obtained through the tooth and there is no evidence of a cellulitis, then antibiotics are not required.

Pain following instrumentation: This is usually due to instruments or irrigants, or to debris being forced into the apical tissues. Occasionally, an acute flare-up of a previously asymptomatic tooth occurs following initial instrumentation so pre-warn patients.

Sclerosed canals: As the incidence of pulp necrosis following canal obliteration is only 13–16%, elective RCT is not warranted. However, where pulp death has occurred, finding the canal orifice may be difficult. If careful exploration with a small file is unsuccessful, investigation of the expected position with a small round bur or ultrasonic tips, aided by magnification and illumination, may help. Success rates of 80% have been reported for canals that were hairline or undetectable on radiographs.

Fractured instruments: The success of removal depends on root canal anatomy and type of the # instrument and location. The fractured file may be bypassed. Sometimes possible to pull out the # portion with fine Steiglitz forceps. If not, insertion of a fine file beside the instrument or ultrasonic tips may dislodge it. Should the # piece be lodged in the apical portion of the canal it may be better to fill the canal coronal to it and keep it under observation, resorting to periradicular surgery as a last-ditch solution.

Perforations: These can be iatrogenic or caused by resorption. Increasingly, MTA is being used for the repair of perforations with excellent results.
- Pulp chamber floor—if small, can cover with MTA or a bioceramic material.
- Lateral perforation—if near the gingival margin, can be incorporated in the final restoration.
- Apical third—usually worth trying a vertical compaction technique.

Ledge formation: Return to a small file curved at the apex to the working length and use this to try and file away the ledge, using EDTA or RC Prep™ as a lubricant.
