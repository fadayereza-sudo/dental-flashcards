The tongue:

Ankyloglossia (tongue tie): The commonest developmental variation. Rx: frenectomy. Current evidence suggests significant improvement in breastfeeding following division.

Fissured tongue: Deep fissuring of the tongue is not pathological in itself (affects 3% of tongues) but may harbour pathogenic microorganisms. Commoner in Down syndrome. Melkerson-Rosenthal syndrome is a deeply fissured tongue in association with recurrent facial nerve palsy and swelling.

Geographic tongue (benign migratory glossitis): A peculiar inflammatory condition of unknown aetiology. Seen in 1–2% of adults. Involves the rapid appearance and disappearance of atrophic areas with a white demarcated border on the dorsum and lateral surface of the tongue. Due to temporary loss of the filiform papillae. Pain is usually 2° to other stimuli, e.g. acidic foods. Rx: reassurance.

Sore tongue (glossodynia): May occur in the presence or absence of clinical changes. Main causes of glossitis are iron deficiency anaemia, pernicious anaemia, candidosis, vitamin B group deficiencies, and lichen planus. Sore, but clinically normal tongue is a common problem and often psychogenic in origin; however, the first line of Rx is to exclude any possible organic cause, e.g. haematological deficiency states.

The lips:

Granulomatous cheilitis (orofacial granulomatosis): Characterized by diffuse swelling of the lips, cheeks, or face and is histologically similar to Crohn's disease. May be the initial manifestation of Crohn's disease or related to sarcoidosis or completely isolated. It may be an allergic reaction to benzoates (toothpaste ingredient) or tomatoes.

Actinic cheilitis: Sun damage to the lower lip causes excessive keratin production and increased mitotic activity in the basal layer. Premalignant. Advise sun blocks and regular follow-up.

Allergic angio-oedema: Severe type I allergic response affecting lips, neck, and floor of mouth. Usually an identifiable cause. Rx: mild—antihistamine PO; severe—as anaphylaxis.

Hereditary angio-oedema: Defect of C1-esterase inhibitor. Lip, neck, floor of mouth swelling. Precipitated by trauma. Diagnosis from family history, bloods: low C4, normal C3, and absence of C1 esterase inhibitor activity. Rx: acute attacks—fresh frozen plasma or partially purified C1-esterase inhibitor. Prophylaxis: long term, tranexamic acid; short term, danazol.

Kawasaki disease: Systemic vasculitis affecting children <5yrs. Criteria include red, dry cracked lips, strawberry tongue, erythematous oropharyngeal mucosa, bilateral conjunctivitis, cervical lymphadenopathy, generalized rash, desquamation of hands and feet, and fever. If suspected, refer to a paediatrician.
