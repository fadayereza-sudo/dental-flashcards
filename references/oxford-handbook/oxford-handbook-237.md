Various parameters affect the choice of Rx for patients with oral cancer; not least among these are the patients themselves, their general health, understanding of their disease, geographical location, and social and domestic commitments.

Suggested management plan:

Establish provisional diagnosis. History, examination, and get to know patient. FBC, U&Es, LFTs (including albumin), bone biochemistry, blood group, ECG, DPT.

Arrange tissue diagnosis by biopsy—usually under LA. Flexible nasendoscopy, head, neck, thorax, imaging (CT/MRI) to exclude synchronous 1° tumour in upper aerodigestive tract (present in at most 15% of cases).

Unless patient has made it very obvious they do not want to know the diagnosis, first inform them, then relatives, fully using the established techniques of breaking bad news with a cancer nurse specialist present.

T1–N0: Surgery or radiotherapy offers a similar cure rate. Surgery is often simpler and with less side effects.

T2 but imaging N0: Have a 30% incidence of occult metastases; majority favour selective neck dissection.

Presence or absence of extracapsular spread in cervical lymph nodes is strongest prognostic indicator of poor outcome.

HPV-16 and -18 have become important causes of oropharyngeal cancer. In non-smokers, stage for stage these cancers have a very much better prognosis. It is entirely preventable by vaccination of pre-sexual children.

5yr survival: 90% for T1–N0, but 30% for T2/3–N1.

Neck lumps: Do not leave chronic cervical lymphadenopathy undiagnosed. A 1° head and neck malignancy must be excluded visually, by imaging or histology (or all three) before biopsy.

Children: An exception; inflammation is common and tumour is rare, so a watch-and-wait policy is reasonable.
