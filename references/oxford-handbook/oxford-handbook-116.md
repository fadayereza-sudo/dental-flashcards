Bridges

Definitions

Bridge (fixed partial denture): A prosthetic appliance that is definitively attached to remaining teeth and replaces a missing tooth or teeth.
Abutment: A tooth which provides attachment and support for a bridge.
Retainer: The part of the fixed prosthesis that is cemented or bonded to an abutment tooth in order to provide retention.
Pontic: The artificial tooth that is suspended from the abutments.

Types of bridge

Fixed–fixed: The pontic is anchored to the retainers with rigid connectors at either end. Both abutments provide retention and support. Both preparations must have at least one common path of insertion.

Fixed–movable: The pontic is anchored rigidly to the major retainer at one end and via a movable joint to the minor retainer at the other end. This design allows some independent movement of the minor abutment and has the advantage that the preparations need not be parallel.

Direct–cantilever: Pontic is anchored at one end of the edentulous span only.

Minimal or no preparation resin-bonded bridges: Retained by resin composite.

Selection of abutment teeth

Assessment of retention: Retention offered by a potential abutment tooth depends on clinical crown height and the available surface area. Larger teeth offer more retention and should be chosen in preference to smaller ones.

Assessment of support: Three factors are important:
- Crown–root ratio. Ideally should be 2:3 but 1:1 is acceptable. As bone is lost, the lever effect on the supporting tissues is ↑.
- Root configuration. Widely splayed roots provide more support than fused ones.
- Periodontal surface area. Ante's law has no scientific basis and no longer has a place in contemporary bridgework design.

Taper and parallelism: Opposing walls of abutments should have a 6° taper or a total degree of convergence of 12°.

Types of pontic

Modified ridge lap: Should make (minimal) contact with the buccal aspect of the ridge. Gives good aesthetics and is the most popular type.
Bullet: Makes point contact with the tip of the ridge. Can be used for posterior bridgework.
Ovate: Aims to address the issue of emergence profile in the maxillary anterior region.
Hygienic: Does not contact saddle, therefore supposedly easy to clean. Unaesthetic, therefore limited to molar replacement.
Saddle (ridge lap): Extends over ridge buccally and lingually, therefore difficult to clean. Should not be used.

Designing bridges
- Assess prognosis of all teeth in vicinity.
- Assess possible abutment teeth (check restorations, endodontic status, periodontal condition, mobility, and take periapical radiographs).
- Select design of retainers. Full coverage is preferred.
- Consider pontics and connectors.
- Where possible, try the least destructive alternative first.

Specific design problems

Periodontally involved abutments: First control periodontal disease. A fixed–fixed type of design is preferable to splint teeth together.

Pier abutments: The central abutment can act as a fulcrum. A stress-breaking element must be introduced, e.g. fixed–movable joint, or avoid pier abutments by simplifying the design.

Tilted abutments: Occurs most commonly following loss of a molar. Options: orthodontic Rx to upright, two-part bridge, telescopic crowns, or precision attachments.

Canines: The canine is often the keystone of the arch, and a very difficult tooth to replace. If a canine is to be replaced with a bridge, the occlusal scheme should be designed to provide group function in lateral excursion—never canine guidance.

Bridge failures

Most common reasons for failure: Loss of retention, mechanical failure (e.g. # of casting), problems with abutment teeth (e.g. 2° caries, periodontal disease, loss of vitality).

Before replacement, careful analysis of reasons for failure is necessary. Minor problems should be repaired if at all possible. Fractured porcelain can be repaired with specialized repair kits. Failure may be due to loss of vitality—RCT can often be carried out through the retainer of the abutment.
