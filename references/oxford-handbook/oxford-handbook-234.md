These are the commonest fractures of the facial skeleton. Most are the result of fights and road traffic accidents.

Classification: The most useful is based on the site of injury: dento-alveolar, condyle, coronoid, ramus, angle, body, symphyseal, or parasymphyseal.

Common fractures: Condylar neck fractures are commonest. Often found with a fracture of the angle or canine region of the opposite side of the jaw. Rarely, bilateral condylar fracture is found with a symphyseal fracture—'guardsman's fracture' from falling on the point of the chin. Angle fracture usually occurs through wisdom-tooth socket, and body fracture commonly through canine socket.

Diagnosis: History of trauma. Ask if the patient can bite their teeth or dentures together in the manner which is normal to them. Inability to do this and a lingual mucosal haematoma is pathognomonic of a mandibular fracture. Look at the face; there is usually bruising and swelling over the fracture site and sometimes lacerations. If the fracture is displaced, there may be gagging on the posterior teeth and the mouth hangs open. The saliva is usually bloodstained. The patient may complain of paraesthesia in the distribution of the IDN. Gentle palpation over the mandible will reveal step deformities, bony crepitus, and tenderness. All have trismus to some degree, opening <40mm.

Examination of the mouth: This may reveal broken teeth or dentures which should be removed. Suction the mouth to clean away blood clots prior to examining both the buccal and lingual sulcus. Look for step deformities in the occlusion, and examine the teeth. Palpate for steps in the lingual and buccal sulcus.

X-rays: DPT and PA mandible are the essentials. Most patients in trauma centres will have additional assessment with a CT scan.

Preliminary Rx: Most patients will be admitted to hospital, nursed sitting up and leaning forward. Keep nil by mouth and maintain hydration by IV crystalloid. Compound fractures need antibiotics.
