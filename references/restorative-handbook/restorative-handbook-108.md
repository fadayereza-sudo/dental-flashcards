## Oral ulceration

Lesions that usually present as painful sores in the oral mucosa and/or the lips. Although they're uncomfortable, most are usually harmless and clear up by themselves within a week or two.

They can arise as a result of a number of disorders such as recurrent aphthous stomatitis and herpes viruses for example. Other causes include adverse reactions to drugs, nutritional deficiencies, some gastrointestinal diseases and, more seriously, oral cancer.

### Signs and symptoms

- Pain; Inflammation; Ulceration; Abnormal appearance; Dehydration (could be present in severe ulceration cases)

### Management

- If there are signs of dehydration (dizziness/lightheaded, tiredness, dry mouth/lips/eyes, fast pulse and respiratory rate, low blood pressure) call the tutor immediately as the patient may need emergency/urgent medical care such as IV fluids
- Determine how long the ulceration has been present

**If ulceration has been present 3 weeks or more:**
- Refer the patient for urgent care via the local rapid access pathway to oral surgery or oral maxillofacial surgery departments for investigation of dysplasia or malignancy

**When ulceration has been present for less than 3 weeks:**

*Recurrent and self-limiting ulceration*
- Advise the patient to use 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthwash, and inform that ulcers are likely to resolve within 1-2 weeks

*Ulceration due to ill-fitting dentures*
- Provide tooth adjustments to address area causing trauma (smoothing sharp edges or temporary filling). Make adjustments to improve denture fit and/or, if appropriate, make arrangements for a new denture to be produced (either by the patient's GDP or internal referral for prosthodontics)

*Ulceration due to trauma from an adjacent tooth or orthodontic appliance*
- Advise the patient to use 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthwash. In case of ortho appliances demonstrate how to use ortho wax over areas which are causing trauma and advise patient to arrange review with GDP who is providing the orthodontic treatment

*Patient has an underlying medical condition or is receiving drug treatment (table below) that might be the cause of the ulcer(s)*
- Referral to the patient's GP. If vesiculobullous disorder is suspected refer to a dermatologist or an oral medicine specialist. In case of primary herpetic gingivostomatitis or herpes zoster infection, if the symptoms are severe or the patient is immunocompromised, consider prescribing antiviral agents (aciclovir or penciclovir), ideally in the early stages

### In all of the above cases

- Recommend optimal analgesia, including topical analgesics (drug therapy described at 'drug prescribing' chapter)
- Do not prescribe antibiotics unless there are signs of spreading infection, systemic infection, or for an immunocompromised patient
- Consider prescribing a topical steroid in case of extensive inflammation or ulceration, discuss this with the tutor, refer to 'Drug Prescribing for Dentistry' (SDCEP, Jan/16) document for guidance on prescribing such medications
- Refer to the local rapid access pathway to investigate potential dysplasia or malignancy if symptoms persist

### Medical conditions and drugs potentially associated with oral ulceration

| Underlying medical conditions | Examples |
|---|---|
| Viral infections | Herpetic stomatitis; Chicken pox; Hand, foot and mouth disease; Herpangina; HIV |
| Bacterial infections | Syphilis; Tuberculosis |
| Mucocutaneous diseases | Lichen planus; Erythema multiforme; Behcet's syndrome; Pemphigoid and variants; Pemphigus vulgaris; Chronic ulcerative stomatitis |
| Haematological diseases | Anaemia; Haematinic deficiencies; Leukaemia; Neutropenia |
| Gastrointestinal | Coeliac disease; Crohn's disease; Ulcerative colitis |
