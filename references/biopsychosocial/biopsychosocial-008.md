## The principle

Being labelled as "ill" or having a health problem has both benefits (access to care, excuse from blame) and costs (stigma, loss of agency, social exclusion). When the costs outweigh the benefits — especially for chronic or visible conditions — people may reject the label and avoid seeking care. Shame about a health condition is itself a barrier to treatment.

## What the book says

The book analyses how illness attribution works: three features typically go together — the person has pain or distress, they have activity limitation, and something is assumed to be wrong with their body or mind. Illness attribution implies the right to access healthcare and excuse from blame. However, it simultaneously risks "disqualification of the person from their full recognition as a self-determining agent" — stigma, reduced social expectations, and exclusion. For chronic conditions where treatment may do "as much harm as good" from the patient's perspective, the costs of the illness label can outweigh the benefits, leading to rejection of the label in favour of "difference" (Ch 4, Sect. 4.2).

## How this applies in dental practice

Dental disease carries unique stigma because the mouth is visible, central to social interaction, and culturally tied to self-care and personal responsibility:

- **Shame drives avoidance.** Many patients with extensive dental disease are profoundly embarrassed. They avoid the dentist not because they don't care but because they fear judgment. The longer they avoid, the worse the disease gets, the greater the shame — a vicious cycle. The first appointment after years of avoidance is one of the bravest things a patient does. Acknowledge it.

- **"I know I should have come sooner."** When a patient says this, they are bracing for blame. Responding with "I'm glad you're here now — let's see what we can do" rather than "yes, you should have" determines whether they come back.

- **Visible dental disease affects identity.** Missing, broken, or discoloured front teeth affect speech, smiling, eating in public, job interviews, relationships. The impact is not vanity — it is a loss of agency in the social world. Understanding this changes how you prioritise treatment: restoring anterior aesthetics may be more important to the patient's quality of life than the clinically "worse" molar.

- **The "non-compliant" patient.** Before labelling a patient as non-compliant, consider: are they avoiding care because of shame? Are they not following advice because they don't understand it, can't afford it, or have different priorities? The BPS model asks you to look beyond the behaviour to the person in their context.

- **Denture stigma.** Some patients experience dentures as a loss of part of themselves. Others refuse to tell family members they have dentures. This is not irrational — it is a response to real social consequences. Treat it with respect.
