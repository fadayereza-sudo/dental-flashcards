## The principle

Behaviour is not simply a matter of knowledge and willpower. It is shaped by biological drives, psychological factors (beliefs, motivation, self-efficacy, emotional state), and social context (norms, resources, support). Telling a patient what to do (brush twice daily, reduce sugar, stop smoking) without addressing the psychological and social factors that maintain their current behaviour is unlikely to produce lasting change.

## What the book says

The book grounds this in self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan): people have innate psychological needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness. Contexts that support these needs facilitate motivation and behaviour change; contexts that undermine them lead to "poorer motivation, performance, and well-being." Agency — the capacity to act and feel in control — is central. Behaviour shaped by one set of environmental conditions may be maladaptive in another, but it was learned for a reason. The social environment profoundly shapes behavioural possibilities: what food is available and affordable, what social norms exist, what support structures are in place (Ch 3, Sects. 3.1–3.2).

## How this applies in dental practice

Oral health instruction (OHI) is one of the most common things a GDP does — and one of the most commonly ineffective. The BPS model explains why, and how to do it better:

- **Information alone doesn't change behaviour.** Almost every patient with caries or periodontal disease *knows* they should brush better and eat less sugar. They are not doing it because of psychological barriers (low motivation, low self-efficacy, depression, overwhelm) and social barriers (cost, routine disruption, family habits, cultural norms). Repeating the same advice louder is not a strategy.

- **Support autonomy, don't prescribe.** Instead of "you need to brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste," try "what do you think would be realistic for you to change?" This invites the patient to own the plan, which is more likely to stick. People who feel controlled resist; people who feel autonomous engage.

- **Build self-efficacy.** Set small, achievable goals. "Can you try using the interdental brushes on just the lower front teeth this week?" is more likely to succeed than "you need to floss every tooth every day." Success builds confidence, which builds further change.

- **Understand the context.** A single parent working night shifts has different barriers from a retired person living alone. "When do you usually brush?" tells you about their routine. "What makes it hard to do that consistently?" reveals the real obstacles.

- **Motivational interviewing principles.** Ask open questions, listen reflectively, affirm what the patient is already doing well, and summarise. Elicit the patient's own reasons for change rather than providing your reasons. "What concerns you about your gum health?" works better than "your gums are bleeding because you don't brush properly."

- **Don't blame.** Moralising about oral hygiene and diet undermines the patient's sense of agency and makes them less likely to engage. People change when they feel supported, not shamed.
