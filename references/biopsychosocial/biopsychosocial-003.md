## The principle

Chronic stress is a key mechanism linking psychosocial adversity to biological disease. It arises when a person faces demands that exceed their resources, especially when the situation feels uncontrollable. The resulting chronic activation of the stress-arousal system causes inflammatory responses and biological damage across multiple organ systems.

## What the book says

Stress is defined as arising from a mismatch between environmental demands and the person's resources to cope — particularly when the situation is perceived as uncontrollable. Lazarus's formulation: "stress is particularly powerful when the individual must struggle with demands that cannot easily be met." Chronic stress leads to persistent activation of the arousal system, which triggers inflammatory responses that adversely affect the immune system and other organs. The Lancet published a major study showing chronic stress carries an attributable risk for cardiovascular disease on par with smoking, hypertension, and diabetes. Importantly, stress is quintessentially biopsychosocial: the psychological sense of agency is compromised because social demands are excessive and resources inadequate, and this chronic psychosocial misfortune causes top-down dysregulation of biological processes (Ch 4, Sect. 4.3).

## How this applies in dental practice

Chronic stress has direct consequences for oral health that a GDP sees regularly:

- **Bruxism and tooth wear.** Stress-driven parafunctional habits cause attrition, abfraction, muscle pain, and fractured restorations. Prescribing a splint without addressing stress is treating the symptom.

- **TMD.** Temporomandibular disorders are strongly associated with psychological stress, anxiety, and catastrophising. Asking about life stressors is part of TMD assessment, not an optional extra.

- **Periodontal disease progression.** Chronic stress suppresses immune function and increases inflammatory markers, accelerating attachment loss. A patient whose periodontal disease is not responding to treatment despite good oral hygiene may be under chronic psychosocial stress.

- **Dry mouth.** Stress and anxiety-related medications (antidepressants, anxiolytics) reduce salivary flow, increasing caries risk. The medication is treating the stress, but its side effect creates new dental disease.

- **Oral habits.** Lip/cheek biting, nail biting, pen chewing — all stress-related habits that a GDP should recognise as signals, not just lesions to note.

When you see these presentations clustering in one patient, consider asking: "How are things going generally? Are you under a lot of pressure at the moment?" The answer may explain why the biology isn't responding to biological treatment alone.
